Attention is a differentiable lookup: given a query, it retrieves a weighted average of values, with weights determined by similarity to keys. Applied to image patches, it lets every part of the image gather evidence from every other part in a single layer.

## Scaled dot-product attention

For matrices of queries $Q$, keys $K$ and values $V$,

$$\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

The product $QK^\top$ produces an $n \times n$ matrix of similarity scores for $n$ tokens. Softmax normalises each row into weights, and multiplying by $V$ produces a weighted average of value vectors.

The scaling factor $\sqrt{d_k}$ is not cosmetic. Without it, the variance of the dot products grows with the dimension, pushing softmax into saturated regions where gradients are tiny. Dividing by the square root keeps the logits at a stable scale.

The $n \times n$ score matrix is the source of attention's cost. For $n$ tokens, both compute and memory are quadratic. At 224x224 with 16x16 patches, $n = 196$, which is manageable. At high resolution with small patches, $n$ grows quickly and attention becomes the bottleneck.

:::single-choice{#attention-scaling} Why divide the attention logits by the square root of the key dimension?

::option[To keep the variance of dot products stable so softmax does not saturate]{#attn-scale .correct explanation="Dot products of independent random vectors have variance proportional to dimension; scaling prevents large logits and vanishing gradients."}
::option[To normalise the value vectors to unit length]{#attn-values explanation="The scaling applies to the query-key logits, not to the values."}
::option[To reduce the number of parameters]{#attn-params explanation="Scaling is a fixed arithmetic operation and does not change parameter count."}
:::

## Multi-head attention

A single attention operation produces one weighted average per token. Multi-head attention runs several in parallel with different learned projections, concatenates the results and projects again:

$$\text{MultiHead}(X) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h)W_O$$

Each head can specialise: one may attend to local texture, another to distant object parts, another to a specific colour relationship. The projections reduce the per-head dimension so that the total cost stays comparable to a single full-width head.

Unlike convolution, attention has no built-in notion of spatial proximity. Position must be injected. Early vision transformers added fixed sinusoidal encodings; most modern ones use learned position embeddings or relative position biases. Some architectures use conditional position encodings generated by a small convolution, which allows variable input resolution.

:::single-choice{#multihead} What is the benefit of multiple attention heads over one head of the same total width?

::option[Different heads can learn different similarity patterns and aggregate different kinds of evidence]{#multihead-specialise .correct explanation="Separate projections let heads specialise in distinct relations, and concatenation combines their outputs."}
::option[It reduces the quadratic cost of attention]{#multihead-cost explanation="Multiple heads split the width, keeping total cost similar; the quadratic dependence on token count remains."}
::option[It removes the need for positional information]{#multihead-position explanation="Attention is permutation-equivariant regardless of head count, so position information is still required."}
:::

## Patch embedding

A vision transformer converts an image into a sequence of tokens. The standard approach splits the image into non-overlapping patches, flattens each, and applies a linear projection. A 224x224 image with 16x16 patches yields 14x14 = 196 tokens, each of dimension 768 in ViT-Base.

A class token is prepended, and its final representation is used for classification. This is borrowed from language models, where a special token aggregates sequence information. Some architectures instead pool all patch tokens, which works comparably.

The patch size controls the trade-off between sequence length and spatial resolution. Larger patches reduce compute but lose fine detail; smaller patches increase both. This is why high-resolution ViTs use hierarchical or windowed attention rather than global attention over all patches.

:::single-choice{#patch-size} What is the main consequence of using smaller patches in a ViT?

::option[More tokens, more compute, and finer spatial detail]{#patch-small .correct explanation="Smaller patches increase sequence length quadratically in attention cost while preserving more local information."}
::option[Fewer parameters in the patch embedding]{#patch-params explanation="Smaller patches mean more tokens, so the projection size typically grows rather than shrinks."}
::option[Guaranteed better accuracy regardless of data]{#patch-accuracy explanation="Finer patches can hurt when data is limited because the model has more tokens and less inductive bias."}
:::

## Hierarchical transformers

Global attention over all patches is expensive at high resolution. Swin Transformer applies attention within shifted local windows and merges patches between stages, producing a hierarchy similar to a CNN. The shifted windows allow information to cross window boundaries without global cost.

The result is a backbone with convolutional-like resolution stages and attention-based mixing, which works well for detection and segmentation. Many modern models combine both: convolutional stems, attention blocks, and convolutional or attention-based decoders.

Hybrid designs reflect a practical conclusion. Convolution is an efficient prior for local structure; attention is a flexible mechanism for long-range relations. Neither dominates across all data regimes and compute budgets.

## Data requirements and training

ViTs have fewer built-in priors than CNNs, so they need more data or stronger augmentation to reach comparable accuracy. The original ViT was trained on hundreds of millions of images; DeiT showed that distillation from a CNN teacher and aggressive augmentation can close much of the gap on ImageNet-scale data.

Training details matter more than in CNNs. Warmup is essential, weight decay is applied broadly, and stochastic depth is common. Layer normalisation is placed before the attention and MLP sublayers (pre-norm), which stabilises deep stacks.

At inference, the quadratic cost can be reduced by windowed attention, sparsity, low-rank approximations or token merging. These methods trade some accuracy for substantial speedups, and they are standard in high-resolution or real-time applications.

:::callout{type="insight" title="Attention is not interpretation"}
An attention map shows where a token drew information, not why the prediction was made. Attention weights can be high for irrelevant tokens and low for decisive ones. Treat visualisations as hypotheses to test, not as explanations.
:::
